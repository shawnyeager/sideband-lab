# Who gets to say no to software

NVIDIA wants to sell the limits on agent software as silicon. Its [Open Agent Safety release](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/28/nvidia-releases.html) puts containment on BlueField-4, NVIDIA's data processing unit. Jensen Huang made the case: "You can't have agents roam around and drift around the company… you have to find a way to container it." NVIDIA pitches the result as "a browser for agents."

On containment, almost everyone already agrees with him. No serious operator wants to give agent software open access to a company's network. The open question is narrower and worth more. When an operator runs software on someone's behalf, who gets to stop it, and who pays when nobody did? Control of non-human action is splitting across four gates, and each gate has an owner: the chip vendor, the OS maker, the operator who writes the liability terms, and whoever holds the wallet. Only two of those owners carry the loss when something goes wrong. Those two are the people courts and counterparties will look to when they decide whose no counted.

Start with the chip vendor. NVIDIA's engineers built something real. Commenters on [Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49879883) also read it as restricted computing with a friendlier name, and as a chipmaker finding one more reason to sell chips. An operator can configure a BlueField card to block a connection. When the operator blocks it, nobody learns whose fault the attempt was, or whether anyone approved the payment behind it. NVIDIA sells containment. NVIDIA does not take on fault.

The OS maker's gate is weaker still, because a user grants permission once and the parties argue about it afterward. Meta is [disputing a user's claim](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/30/meta-disputes-claim-that-muse-read-a-users-private-messages-without-permission/) that its Muse assistant read private messages without permission. The two sides disagree over who held Full Disk Access, and nobody has resolved it. That's the point. A user clicks yes on a settings screen. Later, the user and the vendor look at that same yes and reach opposite conclusions about what it covered.

Operators do better on fault. Eoin Higgins makes the cleanest version of the argument in [There are no rogue AI agents](https://eoinhiggins.substack.com/p/there-are-no-rogue-ai-agents): "'rogue' implies independently deciding to do something that was prohibited, and nothing we know about these incidents suggests that happened." Drop the word rogue and every incident has an operator, the company that deployed the software, set its scope, and connected it to live systems. Of the four gate owners, operators are the only ones whose lawyers put fault in writing before an incident instead of arguing about it after.

Whoever holds the wallet handles the other question: who authorized this spend. Cloudflare has shipped [Machine Payments](https://developers.cloudflare.com/changelog/post/2026-09-30-machine-payments/) on x402, so a developer can attach payment to the HTTP request itself. Patterson at Ceramic put the premise plainly: "Agents need to act on their own behalf, and payments are how they do it." I'd put it the other way around. Payments are how a person decides, in advance and in numbers, how far software runs on their behalf. Setting a spend limit is saying no with a dollar figure attached. [Beltic came out of stealth](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/beltic-emerges-from-stealth-to-secure-autonomous-agent-transactions-at-scale-302893772.html) with seed money to verify both the agent and the transaction, betting that an operator's one bad call, repeated by software at volume, turns into thousands. That is a fundraise, not adoption. Beltic's investors are betting on where operators will put the checkpoint, nothing more.

The case against a fight is that the four owners divide the work. NVIDIA isolates the workload, the OS maker limits access, the operator's lawyers assign fault, and the wallet holder sets a cap. Every serious operator will use all four, and nobody has to win. That's right as engineering and wrong about money. When one owner allows what another should have blocked, someone absorbs the loss, and neither NVIDIA nor the OS makers are volunteering. NVIDIA sells cards. OS makers ship settings. Neither carries the bill when someone stretches a user's grant.

So the person who signs off on spend ends up holding the fault. An operator who signs a spend authorization leaves the clearest record of intent, and counterparties, customers, and courts will reach for that record first. I expect operators to fold their liability terms and their spend controls into one decision: whoever sets the limit owns the outcome. Operators will budget for silicon containment per card, as a cost of doing business. Lawyers will cite OS permission screens as exhibits in disputes. The no that courts and counterparties respect will be the one an operator wrote into a spending policy, and the operator who signed that policy will carry the fault.
