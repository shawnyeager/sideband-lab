# Who gets to say no to software

NVIDIA wants to sell the limits on agent software as silicon. Its [Open Agent Safety release](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/28/nvidia-releases.html) puts containment on BlueField-4, NVIDIA's data processing unit. Jensen Huang made the case: "You can't have agents roam around and drift around the company… you have to find a way to container it."

No serious operator wants agent software loose on a company's network, so on containment almost everyone agrees with him. When an operator runs software on someone's behalf, who gets to stop it, and who pays when nobody did? Control of non-human action is splitting across four gates, and each gate has an owner: the chip vendor, the OS maker, the operator who writes the terms, and whoever holds the wallet. Only the last two can name fault or authorize spend, and only their no counts.

Start with the chip vendor. NVIDIA's engineers built something real. Commenters on [Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49879883) also read it as restricted computing with a friendlier name, and as a chipmaker finding one more reason to sell chips. An operator can configure a BlueField card to block a connection. When the operator blocks it, nobody learns whose fault the attempt was, or whether anyone approved the payment behind it.

The OS maker's gate is weaker still. Meta is [disputing a user's claim](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/30/meta-disputes-claim-that-muse-read-a-users-private-messages-without-permission/) that its Muse assistant read private messages without permission. The two sides disagree over who held Full Disk Access, and nobody has resolved it. A user clicks yes once. Later, the user and the vendor look at that same yes and reach opposite conclusions about what it covered.

Operators do better on fault. Eoin Higgins makes the cleanest version of the argument in [There are no rogue AI agents](https://eoinhiggins.substack.com/p/there-are-no-rogue-ai-agents): "'rogue' implies independently deciding to do something that was prohibited, and nothing we know about these incidents suggests that happened." Drop the word rogue and every incident has an operator, the company that deployed the software and connected it to live systems. Operators are the only gate owners whose lawyers put fault in writing before an incident.

Whoever holds the wallet handles the other question: who authorized this spend. Cloudflare has shipped [Machine Payments](https://developers.cloudflare.com/changelog/post/2026-09-30-machine-payments/) on x402, so a developer can attach payment to the HTTP request itself. Patterson at Ceramic put the premise plainly: "Agents need to act on their own behalf, and payments are how they do it." I'd put it the other way around. Payments are how a person decides, in advance and in numbers, how far software runs on their behalf. [Beltic came out of stealth](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/beltic-emerges-from-stealth-to-secure-autonomous-agent-transactions-at-scale-302893772.html) with seed money to verify both the agent and the transaction, on the view that an operator's one bad call, repeated by software at volume, turns into thousands. That is a fundraise, not adoption: a bet on where operators will put the checkpoint.

The case against a fight is that the four owners divide the work. NVIDIA isolates the workload, the OS maker limits access, the operator's lawyers assign fault, and the wallet holder sets a cap. Every serious operator will use all four, and nobody has to win. That's right as engineering and wrong about money. When one owner allows what another should have blocked, someone absorbs the loss, and neither NVIDIA nor the OS makers are volunteering.

Amazon has already shown what the winning no looks like. Amazon's lawyers wrote agent terms into its [Conditions of Use](https://www.amazon.com/gp/help/customer/display.html?nodeId=GLSBYFE9MGKKQXXM): any agent must identify itself, and Amazon may limit agent access "at our sole discretion," including "by technical measures." Amazon says it asked Meta to remove Amazon from the Muse experience. Then Amazon started blocking Muse on Amazon.com. Muse shoppers got a [popup](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/amazon-blocks-metas-muse-ai-assistant-in-new-standoff-over-agentic-shopping/): "Continued access by an unauthorized AI agent violates Amazon's Conditions of Use, to which our customers have agreed." Same login, same card, same catalog. A customer who clicked checkout could still buy. A customer who sent Muse to do it could not.

Look at what Amazon did in one move. Its lawyers named fault in advance, in terms the customer had already accepted. Its engineers refused the spend at checkout. Nobody at NVIDIA made that call, and nobody waited on a settings screen. Meta and a user are still arguing over a Full Disk Access grant. Amazon settled its own Muse question with a contract and a popup.

The no that sticks doesn't belong to whoever builds the sandbox or ships the toggle. It belongs to a named operator who writes the terms and enforces them, and, when money moves, to the person who authorizes the spend. Anyone building agent software is not bottlenecked on containment. They need two yeses: one from the operator whose terms cover the transaction, and one from whoever holds the card. Meta's Muse users had the card. They never had Amazon's yes.
