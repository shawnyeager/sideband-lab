# Who gets to say no to software

NVIDIA wants the limits on agent software to live in silicon. Its [Open Agent Safety release](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/28/nvidia-releases.html) puts containment on BlueField-4, its data processing unit. Jensen Huang made the case without decoration: "You can't have agents roam around and drift around the company… you have to find a way to container it." NVIDIA pitches the result as "a browser for agents."

Containment is the part everyone already agrees on. Nobody serious argues that agent software should run with open access to a company's network. The open question is narrower and worth more. When software acts on a person's behalf, who gets to stop it, and who pays when nobody did? Control of non-human action is splitting across four gates: the chip, the operator's liability language, the operating system's permission toggles, and the wallet. Only two of those name a person. Those two are where the fight over who gets to say no will settle.

Start with the chip. It is real engineering, and it is also a sales motion. Commenters on [Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49879883) read it as restricted computing with a friendlier name, and as a chipmaker finding one more reason to sell chips. Both readings can be true while the product still works. A BlueField card can block a connection. Blocking a connection says nothing about whose fault the attempt was, or whether anyone authorized the money it was about to move. The chip contains. It does not assign.

The OS toggle is weaker still, because permission is granted once and argued about afterward. Meta is [disputing a user's claim](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/30/meta-disputes-claim-that-muse-read-a-users-private-messages-without-permission/) that its Muse assistant read private messages without permission. The argument turns on Full Disk Access and on who held that permission. It remains unresolved, which is the point. A toggle records that someone clicked yes. It does not settle what the yes covered, and both sides can read the same grant and reach opposite conclusions.

Liability language does better on fault. Eoin Higgins makes the cleanest version of the argument in [There are no rogue AI agents](https://eoinhiggins.substack.com/p/there-are-no-rogue-ai-agents): "'rogue' implies independently deciding to do something that was prohibited, and nothing we know about these incidents suggests that happened." Drop the word rogue and every incident has an operator, the company that deployed the software, set its scope, and connected it to live systems. Operator terms name that company in advance. Of the four gates, liability language is the only one that answers "whose fault" before the incident instead of after.

The wallet answers the other question: who authorized this spend. Cloudflare has shipped [Machine Payments](https://developers.cloudflare.com/changelog/post/2026-09-30-machine-payments/) on x402, which lets a client pay for a resource inside the HTTP request itself. Patterson at Ceramic put the premise plainly: "Agents need to act on their own behalf, and payments are how they do it." I'd put it the other way around. Payments are how a person decides, in advance and in numbers, how far software can go on their behalf. A spend limit is a no with a dollar figure attached. [Beltic came out of stealth](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/beltic-emerges-from-stealth-to-secure-autonomous-agent-transactions-at-scale-302893772.html) with seed money to verify both the agent and the transaction, on the logic that one bad call, executed by software at volume, turns into thousands. That is a fundraise, not adoption. It shows where investors expect the checkpoint to sit, nothing more.

The case against a fight is that these gates stack. The chip contains, the OS scopes, the contract assigns, the wallet caps. Every serious deployment will use all four, and no layer has to win. As engineering, that is correct. As a question of money, it misses what matters. When one gate allows what another should have blocked, someone absorbs the loss, and neither the chip vendor nor the OS maker is volunteering to be that someone. NVIDIA sells cards. OS makers ship settings. Neither business carries the bill when a grant gets stretched.

So fault and spend converge. The party that signs the spend authorization holds the clearest record of intent, and that record is what a counterparty, a customer, or a court will reach for first. I expect operator liability language and the wallet to collapse into a single control: whoever sets the limit owns the outcome. Silicon containment becomes a cost of doing business, priced per card. OS toggles become exhibits in disputes. The no that holds will be written as a spending policy, and the name on that policy will be the name on the fault.
